Video-to-BGM retrieval · stitching step

Keep or switch?

For every scene of a silent video, retrieval gives a ranked list of music windows that fit it. Taking the best one for each scene makes the soundtrack change every few seconds. The Viterbi step picks one window per scene for the whole video at once, trading how well each window fits against how often the music changes.

1 · The three moves

Between two scenes, the music can do one of three things

Every route through the video is a chain of these moves. Each move has a price, paid in the same units as the fit score, so the algorithm can weigh "this window fits better" against "but the music would have to change".

Continue

cost 0

Same song, picking up exactly where the last scene left off. You hear no seam at all.

Jump

cost λjump

Same song, but a different part of it, for example skipping to the chorus. A small seam, same instruments and key.

Switch

cost λswitch + μ·(Δkey + Δtempo)

A different song. The base price λswitch, plus μ times how far apart the two songs are in key and tempo, so clashing changes cost more.

The algorithm, one scene at a time

Viterbi walks left to right. For every box it remembers only the best way to arrive there (the dark arrow) and that route's total score (the pill). At the end it takes the best final box and follows the arrows back. Press Start over and then Next, and click any box to see its arithmetic.

3 · The rule behind it

Why it only needs one pass

Write best(s, w) for the highest total score of any route that ends at window w in scene s. The best route into w must come from some window w′ of the previous scene, and from there it is only worth taking the best route into w′. So:

best(s, w) = fit(w) + maxw′ [ best(s−1, w′) − move(w′ → w) ]

That is the whole algorithm: fill in best column by column, keep an arrow to the winning w′, and read the route back from the best final window. The total it maximizes is

Σ fit of the chosen windows − Σ move costs paid

Why not just try every route? On the real video above there are possible routes. Viterbi does comparisons instead, and still finds the single best one, exactly.

Why not decide scene by scene? Because a good choice now can depend on what comes later. In the example, keeping song A in scene 2 only pays off because a strong A window waits in scene 3. A left-to-right greedy rule cannot see that.

In the code the same thing is written as a cost to minimize, −z + move cost (src/bgm/retrieval/stitch.py). Maximizing score and minimizing cost give the same route.

5 · What the penalty does

Too low flickers, too high misses changes

No penalty (λ = 0): every scene takes its own best window. On the test video the music changes 9 times in 72 s, 6 of them where the film keeps the same song.

Tuned penalty (λswitch ≈ 1): a change has to earn about one standard deviation of fit. The music changes 4 times, and 3 of the 4 are the true changes.

High penalty (λswitch ≥ 3): changing is so expensive that the route holds one song across a real change. Only 2 changes are left, and the 42.8 s change is missed.

Viterbi only chooses among what retrieval offers. Songs S3 and S4 never appear among the candidates of this video, so no setting can pick them. Stitching fixes when the music changes, not which songs retrieval found.

How the values are chosen. Per fold, on validation videos only (never test): a grid over λswitch ∈ {0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, never}, λjump ∈ {0.25, 0.5, 1, 2}, μ ∈ {0, 0.5, 1}, scored by how often the right song plays plus how well music changes line up with true changes.

Result on the test set (title split, 5 folds, 50 joined test videos per fold):

  • music changes within ±1 s of a true change (F1): 0.730 Viterbi vs 0.602 best-per-scene, Δ +0.128 [+0.109, +0.148]
  • changes per minute: 3.4 vs 6.2
  • time the right song plays: 0.073 vs 0.051

Change predictor. The final system replaces the single λswitch with a price for each boundary, from a model that reads the video and estimates the chance p that the music changes there: continue costs −α·log(1−p), switch −α·log p. Most of its gain comes from segment length rather than the picture; see boundary 4 in the predictor view above.
